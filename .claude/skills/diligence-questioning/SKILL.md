# Diligence Questioning

A good question is specific, answerable, and aimed at a fact you cannot get from a document. Everything else wastes the scarcest resource in a deal: time with management.

## Techniques

1. **Document-first filtering** — Delete every question answerable from the CIM, the filings or the data room. Management time is for facts that exist only in their heads and for testing how they think.

2. **Question classification** — Mark each question diagnostic (tests the thesis), confirmatory (verifies a number) or deal-breaking (determines whether we proceed). Sequence so the deal-breaking questions get asked while everyone is still fresh.

3. **Disconfirming design** — Write the questions designed to disprove the thesis, not support it. These are the ones most often dropped when the agenda runs long, and they carry the most information.

4. **Omission analysis** — Read the CIM for what is missing: a metric not disclosed, a period skipped, segments merged, a customer unnamed, a definition changed. Every omission becomes a question, and the reaction to that question is itself data.

5. **Follow-up trees** — For each priority question, pre-write the follow-up for the good answer, the evasive answer and the bad answer. Follow-ups invented in the room are weaker than follow-ups prepared out of it.

6. **Definition interrogation** — Ask what each metric means before asking what it is. Churn, backlog, bookings, recurring revenue and maintenance capex are defined differently by every company, and the definition usually explains the number.

7. **Single-question discipline** — Never compound. A compound question lets the respondent answer the easy half. One question, then silence.

8. **Evidence anchoring** — Ask for the document that supports the answer, in the room. "What would show me that?" converts an assertion into a diligence request and does it politely.

9. **Session design** — Agenda, timing, roles, who asks and who notes, and time explicitly protected for the hard questions. Without protection, the hard questions get ten minutes at the end.

10. **Evasion detection** — Review the transcript for questions not actually answered, definitions changed mid-answer, specific questions given general answers, and deflections to a colleague who is not present. Each is a follow-up, not a conclusion.

11. **Cross-source consistency checking** — Test every answer against the CIM, the financial statements, prior-period filings and any earlier answer. Inconsistency is the highest-yield finding in diligence, and it is only found by someone who reads everything.

12. **Value-impact triage** — Sort findings by their effect on price, structure or the decision to proceed. A findings list ordered by function rather than by value impact buries the three things that matter.

13. **Second-session construction** — Build the follow-up list from the first session's answers, ordered by value impact. The second session is where diligence actually gets done.

14. **Expert-call design** — Define the expert profile, the questions to ask, the questions that cannot be asked for confidentiality reasons, and the validation route for what you are told. Expert calls are evidence, not proof.

## Quality bar

- [ ] No question answerable from documents already held
- [ ] Every question single, specific and answerable
- [ ] Follow-up trees written in advance for the priority questions
- [ ] Definitions asked before numbers
- [ ] Findings ordered by value impact, with each tied to a price, structure or proceed consequence

## Outputs

Prioritised question list by function, session agenda and roles, follow-up trees, answer evaluation, inconsistency log, findings memo ordered by value impact.
