# Industry Analysis

Industry analysis that does not change a number in the model is decoration. Every section must end in a forecast implication.

## Techniques

1. **Two-way market sizing** — Build top-down from published market data and bottom-up from units, customers or capacity. Reconcile the two and state which you trust. A single-method sizing has no error check.
   *Fails when:* a vendor's market-size estimate is cited as fact without knowing its methodology.

2. **Boundary definition** — Define exactly what is inside and outside the market before sizing it. Most disagreements about market size are disagreements about the boundary.

3. **Profit-pool mapping** — Allocate industry profit across value-chain stages, not just revenue. Where profit pools sit tells you which stage to own and which to avoid.

4. **Quantified five forces** — Every force supported by a number: supplier concentration ratios, buyer concentration, observed switching rates, entry examples in the last five years, substitute price-performance trajectories. Adjectives are not analysis.

5. **Share-shift diagnosis** — Track three-year share movement and attribute each shift to the specific strategic action that caused it. Static share tables tell you who is big; share shifts tell you who is winning.

6. **Cost-curve positioning** — Build the industry cost curve and place the subject on it. Position on the curve predicts behaviour through a downturn better than any margin comparison.

7. **Cycle placement with signposts** — Define the cycle's historical length and amplitude, state where we are, and name the indicator that would confirm a turn. Then state what the forecast assumes about the cycle — usually it silently assumes none.

8. **Elasticity quantification** — Express demand sensitivity to GDP, rates, FX and sector-specific drivers as betas with a historical basis. This is what makes a downside case defensible rather than invented.

9. **Pass-through analysis** — Measure historical input-cost pass-through: the percentage recovered and the lag in months. This single measure decides margin behaviour in an inflationary case.

10. **Disruption screening with evidence** — Identify disruption vectors that show measurable early traction — adoption data, funding, regulatory movement — rather than plausible narratives. Most disruption predictions fail the traction test.

11. **Regulatory pathway mapping** — Regulator, rules, pending changes, enforcement posture and the transferability of licences on a change of control. The last point has killed more deals than valuation has.

12. **Scenario construction with signposts** — Three scenarios with named drivers, quantified outcomes and the observable signposts that confirm each. Signposts convert a scenario into something that can be monitored after the deal closes.

13. **Read-across to the forecast** — Translate every conclusion into specific model implications: which line moves, by how much, in which year. This is the step that most industry sections skip and the only one that matters to the valuation.

## Quality bar

- [ ] Every claim sourced with publisher, date and methodology
- [ ] Market sized two ways and reconciled
- [ ] Vendor estimates distinguished from primary data
- [ ] The five most important unknowns stated explicitly
- [ ] Every section ends in a forecast implication

## Outputs

Market sizing and segmentation, profit-pool map, competitive structure analysis, cycle assessment, regulatory map, scenario set with signposts, forecast read-across.
