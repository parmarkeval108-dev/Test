# Screening and Targeting

Screening produces a list. Origination produces a conversation. The gap between them is deliverability, and it is where most acquisition programmes fail.

## Techniques

1. **Universe construction by three routes** — Build from industry classification, from the value chain, and from adjacency rings around the client's core. Each route surfaces targets the others miss; the union is the universe.

2. **Criteria before candidates** — Write and weight the screening criteria before looking at a single company. Publishing the weights first prevents the criteria from being reverse-engineered around a favoured target.

3. **Weighted scorecard with sensitivity** — Score on strategic fit, financial quality, integration difficulty, deliverability and valuation risk. Then show which rankings flip if the weights move ±10 points. A ranking that is not robust to its own weights is not a ranking.

4. **Deliverability as a separate axis** — Score desirability and deliverability independently. Shareholder willingness, board composition, standstills, regulatory posture and competing bidders decide whether a target is actually available. The best target nobody can buy is not a target.

5. **Affordability envelope** — Work backwards from the acquirer's balance sheet: given leverage and rating constraints, define the deal-size envelope, then screen only inside it. This removes the perennial waste of analysing targets the client cannot fund.

6. **Trigger-event monitoring** — Define the observable signals that a target may come to market: CFO or CEO change, auditor change, adviser hire, sponsor fund-life approaching year five, segment reporting changes, a capex pause, a failed prior process. Monitor them systematically rather than reactively.

7. **Ownership and decision-rights mapping** — Identify who actually controls a sale decision: shareholders above 3%, management holdings, shareholder agreements, family arrangements, founder intent. A target is only approachable when you know who to approach.

8. **Prior-process research** — Establish whether the target has run a sale process before: when, who advised, who bid, why it failed, and what would need to be different. A failed process is the single most useful piece of origination intelligence available.

9. **Anti-target discipline** — Maintain an explicit list of targets the client has decided not to pursue with the disqualifying fact for each. This stops the same names recirculating every quarter.

10. **Cost-of-delay assessment** — For each priority target, estimate what changes in twelve months: the likely alternative acquirer, likely price movement, and the optionality lost. This converts a list into a sequence.

11. **Competitive tension mapping** — For each target, name the other credible acquirers and what each would pay for. Identify where the client has a genuine willingness-to-pay advantage — that is where it should compete.

12. **Approach strategy design** — Who calls whom, in what sequence, what is said in the first five minutes, and the fallback if rebuffed. A well-screened target lost to a bad first approach is a complete waste of the analysis.

## Quality bar

- [ ] Criteria and weights published before scoring
- [ ] Exclusions documented, including what was deliberately left out of the universe
- [ ] Deliverability scored separately from desirability
- [ ] Every target inside the client's affordability envelope
- [ ] Anti-target list maintained with reasons

## Outputs

Screening universe, weighted scorecard with sensitivity, tiered target list, one-page target profiles, trigger-event monitor, approach strategy, screening memo.
